- *Rights of Nature, Portugal vs. South Africa* (`field-trips/deskStudy/rights-of-nature-portugal-vs-south-africa.md`): South Africa's National Water Act (1998) created a legally binding "Reserve" — a minimum water allocation prioritized above all other licensed use, split into basic-human-needs and ecological components — written explicitly to correct apartheid-era water dispossession (by 1994, "hardly any Black individual had a formal water resource right"). It's a statutory minimum-allocation mechanism, not legal personhood like Ecuador's or New Zealand's Whanganui River model, and its equity mandate remains substantially unrealized in practice. Portugal and the EU have **no comparable mechanism at all** — no personhood, no binding minimum-flow guarantee (Natura 2000 and the Water Framework Directive are descriptive, not binding-allocation); an EU citizens' initiative for ecosystem rights-holder status was only registered in 2026. Uses that legal gap to help explain why the Greater Côa Valley has had next to no wildlife: mid-20th-century deforestation followed by post-1986 EU-accession land abandonment (some of Europe's highest rural-abandonment rates), sustained historical wolf persecution (down to ~400-500 Iberian wolves peninsula-wide by the 1970s), 337 active ICNF hunting zones with no ecological-priority law subordinating them, and a recovery that depends entirely on voluntary land purchase (Faia Brava, Portugal's only private protected area) rather than any legal floor — the European beaver's 2025 natural return from Spain being the one recovery driver independent of funding or law. Keywords: National Water Act, rights of nature, legal personhood, water governance, Côa wildlife scarcity, hunting zones. Related: [[Safe_and_Just]], [[Conservation]].
- *Wildlife Relations, Portugal vs. South Africa* (`field-trips/deskStudy/wildlife-relations-portugal-vs-south-africa.md`): Compares resistance to conservation across three registers. Active resistance: Côa Valley wolf-livestock conflict (2014-16 attacks, slow/partial compensation, packs disappearing amid farmer frustration) vs. Kruger's unfenced-boundary conflict since 2003 (cattle losses to predators/poachers' snares, legal inability to reclaim strayed livestock) — different shapes of the same underlying cost-absorption question. Historical dispossession: South Africa's Makuleke community was forcibly removed from ~25,000 ha of what's now Kruger in 1969 and won a rare 1998 land-restitution settlement (ownership + tourism-revenue rights + joint management board); the Côa Valley has no honest equivalent — land was abandoned first for unrelated economic reasons, and rewilding organizations bought it afterward from willing sellers, a meaningfully different moral shape the note treats as such rather than forcing a false parallel. Coexistence tolerance: Portugal's own "From Fear to Favor" survey (220 respondents) found hunters neutral-to-positive toward wolves, statistically indistinguishable from the general public — it's livestock owners specifically who carry negative sentiment, complicating the assumption that hunting culture predicts anti-predator hostility. Keywords: Makuleke land restitution, wolf-livestock conflict, Kruger fence removal, hunting zones, coexistence attitudes. Related: [[Conservation]], [[Decolonial_Ecology]].
- *Safari-Scale Upscaling: Côa Valley vs. Limpopo* (`field-trips/deskStudy/safari-upscaling-coa-valley-limpopo.md`): The most numbers-heavy of the project's comparative desk studies, computed directly from the eco-connectivity notebook's own GeoPandas layers. Four barriers to Limpopo-style safari tourism: (i) land-tenure fragmentation — 337 hunting-zone parcels (663,158 ha) held by dozens of separate ZCA/ZCM/ZCT concessions, none for sale as a unit, even though dissolved-contiguous blocks geometrically clear the >100,000 ha "mega-rewilding" literature threshold; (ii) no Big-Five-equivalent species roster (one wild large carnivore, the Iberian wolf, plus semi-wild grazer proxies for extinct megafauna); (iii) a low-road-density interior landscape lacking safari-circuit visitor infrastructure; (iv) 7,184 ha of legally unavailable UNESCO heritage land (Alto Douro Wine Region + the Côa rock-art core) that can never convert to reserve land under any purchase model. Against the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Conservation Area's ~10 million ha, the Côa study area's absolute ceiling is ~10% of that scale — but current actual holdings (Faia Brava, ~857 ha published figure) sit at roughly 0.002-0.009% of GLTFCA scale, and the realistic private-purchase trajectory is decades-long at best. See [[Data]] for the underlying `land_tenure.gpkg`/`heritage_protection.gpkg` vector layers this analysis computes directly from. Keywords: land tenure fragmentation, GLTFCA, mega-rewilding threshold, UNESCO heritage exclusion, hunting-zone cadastre. Related: [[Conservation]], [[Data]].
