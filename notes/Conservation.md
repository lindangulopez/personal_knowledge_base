- *Fontainebleau: Visitor-Pressure Management and Implications for the Greater Côa Valley* (`field-trips/deskStudy/fontainebleau-ecotourism-implications-coa-valley.md`, compiled 25 Aug 2026; companion data/QGIS at `research/fontainebleau-comparison/`): Third in this project's comparative desk-study series, alongside [[Political_Ecology|Safari-Scale Upscaling]] and the Camargue comparison above. Grounded in Linda's own 25 years' residence in Fontainebleau, France, doing volunteer ecological consulting and field-guide work — see [[Professional_Background]] — but explicitly not a dated field-visit report: no site names, dates, or species lists are drawn from memory, only from live Wikidata/OSM/GBIF queries, with the same Sourced/Derived/Inferred discipline used in the Limpopo study above. Core finding: the Forêt de Fontainebleau's core state forest (153.0 km², an unresolved ~2x discrepancy against Wikidata's 280.92 km² figure for the same item, most likely the broader *massif forestier* vs. the core forest alone) carries 17 named Réserves Biologiques (ONF's strict-protection mechanism, split into 8 no-silviculture Réserves Biologiques Intégrales — including the Réserve biologique intégrale de la Tillaie, established 1853, one of Europe's oldest forest reserves — and 9 lighter-touch Réserves Biologiques Dirigées), 8.2% of the forest by area, sitting inside one of Europe's most-visited forests (896 mapped climbing/trail-access features within 2 km of the boundary). Only 1 of those 896 facilities falls inside a strict reserve, but the map shows dense clustering right at several reserve edges — a visible visitor-pressure signal the boundary line alone doesn't capture. Four numbered implications for Côa Valley: a tiered strict-core/managed-buffer zoning model (as Faia Brava's footprint grows) rather than one blunt protected/unprotected line; treating Fontainebleau's edge-clustering as an early-warning template for planning buffer zones before infrastructure gets built at a boundary by default; a third independent confirmation of the designation-stacking pattern already flagged in the Camargue entry (state forest + Regional Nature Park + UNESCO Biosphere Reserve + strict-reserve network); and evidence that mass recreational tourism and strict biodiversity conservation can run on the *same* land via access tiering, rather than requiring physical zone separation. Keywords: Fontainebleau, visitor pressure, tiered protection, Réserve Biologique, designation stacking, ecotourism density. Related: [[Data]], [[Professional_Background]].

- *Mine Restoration and Ecological Connectivity in Limpopo Province* (`field-trips/deskStudy/limpopo-mine-restoration-connectivity.md`, compiled 25 Aug 2026; companion data/QGIS at `research/limpopo-mine-restoration/`): A remotely-derived geospatial reassessment, explicitly **not** a field-visit report, of the South African province where Linda held regulatory oversight 1997-2002 — see [[Professional_Background]]. States its evidence tiers up front: Tier 1 documented (cited, not derived), Tier 2 inferred (this study's own spatial reasoning, stated as such), Tier 3 hypothesis — with no "Observed" category at all, since no site visit occurred. Headline finding: Palabora copper mine and its Phalaborwa tailings sit 3.0 km and 3.8 km from Kruger National Park's boundary. Tier 1: two documented transfrontier mechanisms already stitch the province's protected areas across borders — the Great Limpopo Transfrontier Park (Kruger + Mozambique's Limpopo National Park + Zimbabwe's Gonarezhou, fences removed since 2003, ~10M ha total per the figures already verified in the Safari-Scale Upscaling note) and the Greater Mapungubwe TFCA (Mapungubwe + Botswana's Northern Tuli + Zimbabwe's Tuli Circle). Tier 2: 115 protected-area pairs under a stated (not species-calibrated) 40 km centroid threshold, flagged as candidate linkages worth field investigation, not corridors. Tier 3: 24 mines within 10 km of a protected-area boundary (10 within 5 km) — a distance-based screening list, explicitly not an established fragmentation finding, topped by the Phalaborwa cluster given its Tier-1 adjacency. A land-tenure/governance note worth surfacing on its own: Makuleke, a community-conservation area inside Kruger's northern section returned under South Africa's land-restitution programme while remaining under conservation management — see [[Political_Ecology]]. Recommends treating the study strictly as a screening tool (where to send a field team, not what to plan from), pursuing the official SAPAD data path before any planning-grade use, and commissioning a proper resistance-surface model for the Phalaborwa cluster specifically, of the kind already running for the Côa Valley in `research/eco-connectivity/`. See [[Data]] for the two structured datasets (mining sites, protected areas) this study built from scratch, including a flagged Wikidata data-quality error. Keywords: mine restoration, transfrontier conservation, Kruger National Park, evidence tiers, connectivity screening, Palabora. Related: [[Political_Ecology]], [[Data]], [[Professional_Background]].

- *Restoration Ecology — Early View Alert (24 Aug 2026)*: Wiley content-alert email for the journal *Restoration Ecology*, four new open-access Version-of-Record articles. Chouairi et al. (e70534) develop a satellite-remote-sensing screening method for assessing constructed infill health in the Venice Lagoon, meant as a first-pass triage tool for restoration practitioners — see [[Remote_Sensing]]; they argue monitoring should combine seasonal/tidal imagery with elevation, hydroperiod, sediment, and connectivity data rather than vegetation cover alone. Zhou et al. (e70536) study soil seed bank dynamics in China's desert-loess transition zone, finding vegetation patches act as "biological islands" that trap seed, and recommending supplemental seeding where sandy soils limit natural recruitment. Thurston et al. (e70532) test seed-mix richness and soil amendments in Ohio post-agricultural prairie restoration: richer mixes raised species richness but not diversity, and soil amendments (mulch, inoculation) had negligible effect — restoration outcomes were dominated by stochastic establishment of "weedy" natives instead. Wilhelm et al. (e70533) find frequent, late-season prescribed fire produces the most floral resources for bees in a Cumberland Plateau oak-pine savanna restoration, including bee species not previously documented in Tennessee. Keywords: restoration ecology, remote sensing, soil seed bank, prairie restoration, prescribed fire, pollinators. Related: [[Remote_Sensing]].

- *Douro vs. Côa: The Dam Cascade as Backdrop to the 1996 Cancellation* (`field-trips/deskStudy/douro-vs-coa-tourism-dam-counterfactual.md`): Corrects a draft storyMap figure ("141 dams" on the Douro — not a real citable number) with two real ones: ~15 major dams on the Douro's own mainstem vs. ~1,200 dams/weirs across the whole basin (Cortes et al. 2019). - *Movimento para a Salvaguarda das Gravuras de Foz Côa* (`field-trips/deskStudy/movimento-salvaguarda-gravuras-foz-coa.md`): Full history of the 1994-96 civic movement that stopped the planned Barragem de Foz Côa (a separate structure from the completed, unrelated Pocinho Dam on the Douro — a mix-up the note flags explicitly). Timeline: engravings found 1991, made public Nov 1994; a Vila Nova de Foz Côa secondary-school movement forms early 1995 under the slogan "As gravuras não sabem nadar" ("The engravings can't swim"); António Guterres commits to halting the dam if elected (Mar 1995) and does; two petitions gather 70,000+ (claimed closer to 200,000) and ~11,000 signatures; a 70-day encampment outside Jerónimos Monastery; Council of Ministers Resolution nº 14/1996 formally suspends construction (17 Jan 1996); UNESCO World Heritage inscription follows Dec 1998. The unresolved coda: an abandoned 32m cofferdam from the cancelled project was never demolished, and still periodically floods 119 rock-art panels — over 500 cumulative days since 1996 — with demolition (est. €4.5M+) still not completed as of the most recent reporting. Keywords: Foz Côa dam, anti-dam movement, rock art, UNESCO World Heritage, cofferdam, civic protest.

- *Côa Valley Eco-Connectivity v6* (`coa_eco_connectivity_story_v6.ipynb`, Rewilding Portugal / Solo Field Illustrator project's scientific track, 9.03 MB / 66 cells): Analysis notebook modeling ecological connectivity across the Greater Côa Valley for three movement guilds — land (wolf, wildcat, red deer), water (otter, native fish, pond turtle), and air (griffon vulture, Egyptian vulture, golden eagle). Method follows Prima et al. (2024): Random Forest suitability/resistance surfaces built from GBIF occurrence records and environmental covariates, run through Omniscape circuit-theory connectivity, then combined into a species-count-weighted multispecies connectivity surface, layered with a fire-history penalty (2025 wildfires), field-observed barriers and UNESCO heritage-zone protections, land-tenure context, and road/waterway barrier-vs-access classification — all carried forward unchanged from v5 (already logged in `notes/log.md`). Explicitly framed around "safe and just" and flags its own method limits (training accuracy isn't validation; no held-out movement-tracking data). v6 folds in condensed versions of three comparative desk studies already logged as their own entries above — the Limpopo mine-restoration study, the Fontainebleau visitor-pressure study, and the Camargue ecotourism study — plus two genuinely new analyses run outside the notebook's own pipeline: (1) a **naturalist-livability score** for 251 apartment buildings across Guarda, Pinhel, and Vila Nova de Foz Côa (the only municipalities with apartment stock among five checked), weighted for nature/river access (30%), river-corridor driving distance to two field anchor points (25%), amenities (25%), parking (15%), and transit (5%); scores 16.9–78.5, mean 62.0, top buildings clustering in Guarda's urban core, with rankings stable across alternative weightings (Spearman's rho 0.89–0.96); and (2) a **visitor-access analysis** computing real OpenRouteService driving distance/time from Guarda to the three field-GPS-confirmed rewilding sites — Faia Brava (56 km, 70 min), Ermo das Águias (53 km, 52 min), Vale Carapito (39 km, 55 min) — plus an off-road-track-proximity indicator (18–29% of each route within 60m of an unpaved track) as a rough ruggedness proxy. A flagged-not-silently-fixed data-quality find: the livability study's own Vale Carapito anchor point sits ~20 km from this project's field-recorded GPS for the same site, an apparent geocoding error caught by cross-checking against both the field GPS and an independent third source (birdingplaces.eu, agreeing to within ~2 km); the notebook's own Guarda-access analysis uses the field-verified point instead. Outputs (rasters, maps, QGIS project) feed the closing chapter of the project's StoryMap.
