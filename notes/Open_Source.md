- *Keynote — April Johnson: "Social Infrastructure: The Human Work Behind Open Scientific Computing"* (RSECon26 keynote, Day 3, 11 Sept 2026, 09:15-10:00, Lecture Theatre 1, The Wave, session #226; `raw/File (1).md`; April Johnson, Community and People Lead at [2i2c](https://2i2c.org/) — also serves on the Jupyter Community Building Committee and the PyOpenSci Executive Council): 2i2c builds open, interactive computing infrastructure (JupyterHub, Jupyter Book) for research/education communities on the premise that science and education impact is mostly social, not technical — so the work centres on building network effects *between* member communities rather than solving each one's needs in isolation. The keynote's central claim: connection-building — the "soft skills" work of understanding, trust, and moving ideas into action — is not secondary to technical infrastructure, it *is* infrastructure. Draws on 2i2c's community-driven model and stories from the Jupyter/open-scientific-Python communities to walk through five concrete practices for turning big community visions into reality, arguing "soft skills" deserve a seat at the table in technical-infrastructure work rather than being treated as a nice-to-have. Keywords: social infrastructure, 2i2c, JupyterHub, community building, open scientific computing. Related: [[Reproducible_Science]].

- *Training the Trainers: Community-Led Bioinformatics Training with Bioconductor Across Africa* (RSECon26 talk, Day 1, 9 Sept 2026, 13:30-13:45, Lecture Theatre 2, The Wave, session #146; `raw/File (1).md`, [Zenodo record 22225675](https://zenodo.org/records/22225675), `raw/Training the Trainers_ Community-led bioinformatics training with Bioconductor across Africa.md`, `RSEcon26 presentation.pdf`; Laura (Nyasita) Ondari — IITA, Kenya; Trushar Shah — IITA; Maria Doyle — University of Limerick/Bioconductor): Bioconductor's 2025 African training programme delivered three in-person workshops (Kenya, Ethiopia, Benin) in R programming, reproducible workflows, and bulk RNA-seq analysis to 83 researchers, backed by French-translated materials and newly Carpentries-certified local instructors — the same community-led, local-instructor-development model as an explicit design choice for sustaining computational-research capacity in under-resourced settings, rather than one-off parachuted training. Participant demand for more advanced topics (single-cell analysis, metagenomics, CRISPR analysis, workflow management) fed a 2026-2027 roadmap: package-author-led online seminars, a pilot advanced metagenomics workshop co-designed with regional partners in Addis Ababa, and clearer advanced-training pathways. Keywords: Bioconductor, Africa, bioinformatics training, Carpentries, local instructor capacity. Related: [[Reproducible_Science]].

- *Experiments in Upskilling Researchers* (RSECon26 talk, Day 1, 9 Sept 2026, 13:45-14:00, Lecture Theatre 2, The Wave, session #24; `raw/File (2).md`, [Zenodo record 22667508](https://zenodo.org/records/22667508), `raw/Experiments in Upskilling Researchers.md`, `Experiments in Upskilling Researchers v2.pdf`; Esther Turner — National Oceanography Centre): Two years of the NOC Software Engineering and AI team's effort to raise digital skills org-wide — roughly monthly workshops and code surgeries, curated self-serve material, and newer short single-topic sessions, delivered online, in person, and hybrid. - *Following Scrum with part-time teams: Experiences of delivering high-quality Git and GitHub documentation for Met Office employees, partners and collaborators* (RSECon26 talk, Day 1, 9 Sept 2026, 14:00-14:30, Lecture Theatre 2, The Wave, session #199; `raw/File (3).md`, [Zenodo record 22648889](https://zenodo.org/records/22648889), `2026-09-09 - RSECon26.pdf`; Emma Hogan, Philip Whybra, Mark Simpson, James Frost, Andrew Clark, Ben Fitzpatrick, Dimitrios Theodorakis, Jed Baker, Violet Sherratt — Met Office): The Met Office's Science Git Migration Project (launched June 2023, moving 20+ years of Subversion/Trac history to Git/GitHub) spun up a volunteer "Git and GitHub Champions" community; this talk covers the Champions' documentation sub-team specifically, which follows Scrum despite every contributor being part-time and voluntary — engaging only a subset of Scrum Events/Commitments by necessity. Nearly a year of experience, including a two-month stretch with full-time contributors as a contrast condition, covers how availability levels affected communication, planning, and review, and how the team balanced content quality, momentum, and contributor wellbeing without dedicated project time. Keywords: Scrum, part-time teams, Git migration, GitHub documentation, Met Office, volunteer community. Related: [[Reproducible_Science]].

- [Open Source Observatory (OSOR)](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor): The European Commission's collection/knowledge hub for public sector free and open source software — news, events, a Knowledge Centre, OSPO/open-source-governance guidance, an interactive country resource map, and a newsletter. - [Public Code Observatory launches during UN Open Source Week](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/open-source-observatory-osor/news/public-code-observatory-launches-during-un-open-source-week) (Sofia Linna, published on OSOR, 3 Sept 2026): Software Heritage, UNESCO, and the UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies (ODET) launched the **Public Code Observatory** during UN Open Source Week (22–26 June), a global evidence base on open source participation by public institutions. Its first output, *The State of Public Code*, covers roughly 288,400 unique public repositories across all 193 UN member states, built on the Software Heritage Archive (29+ billion source files, ~438 million projects). Key findings: contributor headcount tracks the UN E-Government Development Index more closely than GDP per capita (correlation, not cause); the Americas and Europe hold the largest contributor shares, with growing activity in Australia, Türkiye, Japan, and Singapore; cross-border collaboration remains thin, clustering around a few pairs (UK–US, Australia–US, Brazil–US). Linda drafted a LinkedIn post on this launch (below). Keywords: Public Code Observatory, State of Public Code, UN Open Source Week, Software Heritage, UNESCO, ODET. Related: [[Reproducible_Science]], [[OSPO-RADAR]] (private).

> Open source that governments actually use, mapped for the first time
>
> Open Source Observatory (OSOR) has been the place to track EU public sector open source for years. What's new is the Public Code Observatory, launched during UN Open Source Week (22-26 June) by Software Heritage, UNESCO and the UN Office for Digital and Emerging Technologies. I work in Software Heritage's Open Science division, so I've been watching this one closely.
>
> Its first output, The State of Public Code, replaces assumption with a dataset: (i) roughly 288,400 unique public repositories, (ii) all 193 UN member states represented via government domains, (iii) built on the Software Heritage Archive, over 29 billion source files and 438 million projects.
>
> Two findings are worth sitting with. First, contributor numbers track the UN E-Government Development Index far more closely than GDP per capita, correlation not causation, but it says wealth isn't the main gate to participating in public tech. Second, cross-border collaboration is thin. The Americas hold the largest contributor headcount, Europe the second, and countries like Australia, Türkiye, Japan and Singapore are producing more, but the shared projects that do exist cluster around a handful of pairs (UK-US, Australia-US, Brazil-US). Governments are building in parallel more than they're building together.
>
> That gap is the actual opportunity here. If you work inside a public sector OSPO, or you're deciding whether your government's next platform gets built in the open, what would make you collaborate across a border rather than start from zero again?
>
> Linda Angulo Lopez, 5 September 2026
