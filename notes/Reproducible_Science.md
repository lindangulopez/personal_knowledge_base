- *Côa connectivity pipeline — a Linux-style, FAIR, AI-disclosing collaboration workflow* (`raw/coa-connectivity-lab.md`, [coa-connectivity-lab/eco-connectivity-workflow](https://github.com/coa-connectivity-lab/eco-connectivity-workflow)): The pure-Python Côa Valley connectivity pipeline (see [[Conservation]]) is being opened to collaborators, and the contribution rules are themselves a reproducibility artefact. The workflow copies the Linux kernel model — `main` is branch-protected, nothing is committed to it directly, every change is a rebased branch plus a reviewed pull request, and every commit carries a `Signed-off-by` trailer (Developer Certificate of Origin, `git commit -s`) so code provenance stays clean. On top of that: reproducibility gates in `CONTRIBUTING.md` (no data files in Git, data only via cited `acquire_*` scripts, pinned `environment.yml`, sensitive-species locations generalised to 5 km, every output-changing run logged in `reproducibility-logs`), FAIR4RS metadata (`CITATION.cff`, `codemeta.json`, semantic-version tags archived to Zenodo/Software Heritage and cited by SWHID — ties to the CodeMeta/SWHID entries below), and a mandatory **AI-assistance disclosure block** on every pull request: which files or functions were AI-generated, the prompt or approach, and what the human author verified and how. That block is a working instance of the disclosure-threshold and placement recommendations Linda submitted as GFRN topic lead (see [[Professional_Background]]) — disclose AI use that substantively shapes a result, put it where a reviewer reads it, record human verification rather than implying the tool carries responsibility, and prefer a version-controlled SWHID-able history over a prose statement. Keywords: collaboration workflow, DCO, branch protection, FAIR4RS, AI disclosure, SWHID, reproducibility gates. Related: [[Conservation]], [[Agentic_Coding]], [[Professional_Background]].

- *WCRI 2026 Focus Track — "Towards a Global Reporting Standard for AI Disclosure in Research"* (`raw/WCRI2026_FocusTrack_2B.pdf`, `raw/WCRI2026_FocusTrack_4B.pdf`): Slide decks for a two-part Focus Track session at the 9th World Conference on Research Integrity (WCRI 2026, Vancouver, BC, 3-6 May 2026), presented by Bert Seghers (VCWI/ENRIO/Embassy of Good Science), Kari D. Weaver (OCUL/University of Waterloo), and Mike Perkins (British University Vietnam), ambassadored by COPE, WCRI, STM, the International Science Council, and the Global Young Academy. Part 1 (2.B) frames this as the ninth in a lineage of WCRI Focus Track outputs going back to the 2010 Singapore Statement on Research Integrity — via the 2013 Montreal Statement, 2017 Amsterdam Agenda, 2019 Hong Kong Principles, 2022 Cape Town Statement, and 2024 Athens Statement — with WCRI 2026's own output named the **"Vancouver Standard" for AI Disclosure in Research**. Makes the case for disclosure via a "car, not a hammer" framing (AI's value depends on rules, skills, and shared responsibility built around it, not the tool alone), names today's gap as AI disclosure being expected but discouraged by stigma and fear (leading to under-disclosure), and sets scope: all AI tools and agents (not just LLMs), peer-reviewed articles only for now, drafted in English with an eventual 6-UN-language rollout. Structures the open questions as thresholds (what/when to disclose), placement (where), taxonomy (how to structure), and responsibility/accountability. Part 2 (4.B) repeats the same speakers in different presenting order and adds a "participatory process" framing: developing shared understanding and "harmonisation" across sectors, disciplines, and continents through three consultation rounds (1: needs/structure; 2: key questions of what/where/how; 3: feedback on a draft), explicitly framed as a political-alignment effort rather than a research project. This is the same initiative Linda is contributing to as a GFRN AI Working Group topic lead — see the GFRN entries below and [[Professional_Background]]. Keywords: WCRI 2026, Vancouver Standard, AI disclosure, research integrity, Focus Track, COPE, STM. Related: [[Professional_Background]], [[Machine_Learning]].

- *Consultation Round 2 — Preparatory Reading, v4.5* (`raw/WCRI2026FT_PrepReadingRound2_v45 (1).pdf`, published 8 July 2026, authored by Kari D. Weaver, Kiera McNeice, Mike Perkins, Natalya Tsibulyak, Sergio Santamarina, Lorelei Lingard, and Bert Seghers): The official consultation document for Round 2 (July-October 2026) of the same global AI-disclosure reporting-standard initiative described above, distributed to stakeholders (including GFRN) ahead of their written responses. Summarises what Round 1 established — principles of transparency and accountability don't change because of AI; a disclosure standard should foster reflection rather than become a box-ticking or policing exercise; disclosure should be proportionate, structured yet flexible, inclusive of under-resourced research communities, and treated as a living/adaptable standard — then poses Round 2's five open questions in detail: (1) thresholds, proposing a qualitative (not quantitative) test — disclose when AI use "substantively shapes or replaces human judgement, or has another substantive influence on research processes or its reporting," operationalised by asking whether a disciplinary peer would need to know about the AI use to assess the work's reliability, validity, or reproducibility; explicitly excludes routine spellchecking/grammar/formatting from the disclosure requirement, while flagging that cumulative minor uses can still cross the threshold; (2) placement, laying out integrated-in-context disclosure (dispersed through Methods/results/figure captions) versus a separate centralised AI-disclosure statement, with tradeoffs for each (contextual detail and existing-practice fit vs. easy comparability and support for disclosure that doesn't map onto existing article sections); further pages (not fully captured here) continue into taxonomy, mandatory disclosure, and responsibility/accountability. This is the document Linda's own GFRN input entry below responds to directly. Keywords: consultation round 2, AI disclosure thresholds, disclosure placement, qualitative threshold, WCRI, GFRN. - *GFRN AI Working Group — Input on the Global AI Reporting Standard* (`raw/GFRNAIWG-InputontheGlobalAIReportingStandard.md`, created 28 Aug 2026 by Barbara Templ/GFRN): The shared working document collecting individual GFRN members' contributions to Round 2 of the consultation above, ahead of synthesis into a consolidated GFRN response. Linda's own submitted contributions (credited to "SWH & Rewilding Portugal"), matching her topic-lead sign-up noted in [[Professional_Background]]: on **thresholds** (Topic 1) — define "substantive" functionally rather than numerically, tying the threshold to whether AI use changed the computational path from data to result, covering research design, data collection/cleaning, analysis, model selection, interpretation, or output generation; propose a three-tier model (Level 1 routine/no disclosure, Level 2 material-contribution disclosure, Level 3 disclosure plus enhanced provenance for AI-generated pipelines and multi-step agents, where she argues a version-controlled, Software-Heritage-archived history of the code — an SWHID — is a firmer reproducibility basis than a prose statement); and caution against making full prompt disclosure mandatory, limiting it to cases where the prompt itself substantially determines the result or is the research method. On **placement** (Topic 2) — route research-process AI use to the Methods section, with source-code disclosure resolving to a SWHID for the exact archived AI-assisted code state (cited like a dataset DOI), recommending interoperability with Software Heritage, SciCodes, and CodeMeta rather than a parallel AI-provenance ecosystem. On **taxonomy** (Topic 3) — classify AI use by function and by point in the research workflow, since a function/workflow-based taxonomy stays stable as tools change. The document also records Nicolas Rougier flagging that a taxonomy needs to separate long-standing, unproblematic ML use from generative AI, and citing [Cui & Alexander's LLM-reproducibility paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14349) (see entry below) as motivation for requiring full prompt disclosure when prompts substantially determine outcomes. Builds directly on the GFRN AI Working Group entry below and [[Professional_Background]]'s topic-lead entry. Keywords: GFRN, AI disclosure thresholds, SWHID, CodeMeta, taxonomy, prompt disclosure. - *Cui & Alexander (2026), "Same Prompt, Different Outcomes: Evaluating the Reproducibility of Data Analysis by LLMs"* (`raw/2602.14349v1.pdf`, [arXiv:2602.14349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14349), 15 Feb 2026): A systematic evaluation of whether LLM-generated data analysis reproduces consistently. The authors run 480 total execution attempts — six models across three providers (Claude Haiku 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.6, GPT-5-mini and GPT-5.2, Gemini Flash 3 and Gemini Pro 3), four temperature settings (0.0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0), two prompting strategies (single-step vs. multi-step with intermediate validation), and ten independent executions per configuration — on a realistic five-step pipeline (consolidating government-appointment records for a Canadian province, identifying reappointment patterns, aggregating to analytical units, running an OLS regression, and producing visualizations), scored on completion, concordance with a human-conducted analysis, output validity, and cross-run consistency. Three main findings: single-step prompting has higher completion rates than multi-step, because in multi-step pipelines an error at any stage blocks all later stages (e.g. Gemini Flash 3 hits 100% completion single-step but 0% multi-step, failing every run at Step 2 on file-path handling); generated code is almost always structurally valid but diverges from the human analysis in underspecified data-preparation choices (sorting, missing-value handling, record inclusion); and these data-preparation differences propagate into the analysis, producing distinct clusters of estimated regression slopes that vary in both sign and magnitude — meaning identical prompts, data, and even temperature-zero settings can support different substantive conclusions. The paper's own AI-usage statement discloses that AI assisted with coding, data cleaning, and figure/table generation, with all code and results reviewed by the human authors. Cited by Nicolas Rougier in the GFRN consultation input above as motivating a requirement to disclose prompts when they substantially determine the result. Keywords: LLM reproducibility, data analysis, many-analysts, non-determinism, prompt engineering, AI disclosure. Related: [[Machine_Learning]], [[Professional_Background]].

- *GFRN AI Working Group — First Meeting & ISC/WCRI Consultation Workplan* (26 Aug 2026 meeting, materials dated 28 Aug 2026; `raw/FirstMeeting - 28.08.2026.../` — agenda, meeting summary, presentation slides, recording/transcript; `raw/GFRNAIWG_TopicLeadsandWorkplan.docx`, updated/re-exported as `raw/GFRNAIWG_TopicLeadsandWorkplan.md`; supplemented from a tl;dv AI meeting summary/full transcript of the same session, `raw/New_Notes.md`): GFRN (the Global Federation/Forum of national and regional Reproducibility Networks — DKRN Denmark, BrRN Brazil, UARN Ukraine, SwissRN, AREN Africa, and others) launched an AI Working Group, introduced by Emmanuel Boakye (AREN) as a standing forum on AI and research more broadly, not a one-off reaction. Barbara Templ (GFRN Coordination Office, hosted by SwissRN) and Emmanuel Boakye noted that GFRN colleagues (across the UK, French, and Swiss RNs — a UK colleague reportedly asked "which organisations are responsible for defining these standards?") had already been discussing an AI/reproducibility taxonomy before the ISC initiative launched, so the working group joined rather than reacted to it. The meeting didn't converge on a final disclosure model but surfaced the issues GFRN's contribution should address: a workable, discipline-sensitive definition of "AI" that covers agentic/generative tools without conflating them with decades-old ML methods (raised by Nicolas Rougier; see [[Machine_Learning]]); a distinction between the *threshold* for disclosure and the *degree/scale* of AI involvement once crossed (Oleksandr Berezko, UARN, pressed this as a two-step distinction — first whether to report, then how); keeping reproducibility questions (what's needed to understand/verify a result) conceptually separate from authorship/assessment questions (how much credit an individual gets — Olavo Amaral, BrRN, framed this distinction explicitly: AI provenance barely matters for reproducibility/reading an article, but matters a great deal for evaluating a thesis candidate); One contributor (Ephrem Tesfaye Mihretie) described his own practice: writing out in plain language what he gave an AI tool, what it produced, and what he then revised/checked. - *Beyond FAIR Data: Making Research Software a First-Class Research Output* (`raw/2026-06-11-SNS-EELISA.pdf`): A 59-slide course/talk by Roberto Di Cosmo (Director, Software Heritage; Inria/Université Paris Cité), delivered 11 June 2026 — the filename suggests it was given for the EELISA European university alliance. Builds on a Nature commentary (Oct 2025, doi:10.1038/d41586-025-03196-0) calling for software to get the same archive/reference/citation/credit treatment as data, and frames the whole talk as "the how-to behind that call." Opens from Popper's reproducibility principle to argue that preserving source code itself — not just describing methods — is what makes computational research verifiable. Core technical content: the SWHID (Software Hash Identifier), an intrinsic, content-derived identifier built from a Merkle DAG of a project's full development history, which anyone can recompute offline to verify an archived copy matches the original ("a DOI answers 'which deposit?'; a SWHID answers 'exactly which bytes, and prove it'"); practical tooling for researchers — the CodeMeta Generator and an auto-derived `CITATION.cff` for describing software, and the HAL/Software Heritage curated-deposit workflow used in French research; adoption evidence, including a Spinellis et al. MSR 2026 dataset citing 30 SWHIDs and a population-genetics paper embedding SWHIDs since 2020. Closes on policy stakes: the 2019 EU Copyright Directive's "upload filter" rules, where Software Heritage helped secure a non-profit-archive exclusion, and the 2021 US Executive Order's software-supply-chain-security ("Know Your SoftWare") provisions — plus an infrastructure timeline from the archive's 2015 launch through its 2026 ENEA institutional mirror. Keywords: SWHID, CodeMeta, FAIR software, research software citation, Merkle DAG, software supply chain. Related: [[Professional_Background]], [[Data]].
