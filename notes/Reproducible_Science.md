- *GFRN AI Working Group — First Meeting & ISC/WCRI Consultation Workplan* (26 Aug 2026 meeting, materials dated 28 Aug 2026; `raw/FirstMeeting - 28.08.2026.../` — agenda, meeting summary, presentation slides, recording/transcript; `raw/GFRNAIWG_TopicLeadsandWorkplan.docx`): GFRN (the Global Federation/Forum of national and regional Reproducibility Networks — DKRN Denmark, BrRN Brazil, UARN Ukraine, SwissRN, AREN Africa, and others) launched an AI Working Group, introduced by Emmanuel Boakye (AREN) as a standing forum on AI and research more broadly, not a one-off reaction. Its first concrete task is contributing a distinct GFRN reproducibility perspective to the ISC (International Science Council)-led global reporting standard for AI disclosure in peer-reviewed research — a joint initiative with COPE, STM, and the Global Young Academy, running three consultation rounds; Round 2 (Jul-Oct 2026, current) covers five areas: disclosure thresholds, placement, taxonomy, mandatory/non-empty disclosure, and responsibility/accountability. The meeting didn't converge on a final disclosure model but surfaced the issues GFRN's contribution should address: a workable, discipline-sensitive definition of "AI" that covers agentic/generative tools without conflating them with decades-old ML methods (see [[Machine_Learning]]); a distinction between the *threshold* for disclosure and the *degree/scale* of AI involvement once crossed; the difficulty (and likely domain-specificity) of quantitative measurement, given unreliable AI-detection tools; keeping reproducibility questions (what's needed to understand/verify a result) conceptually separate from authorship/assessment questions (how much credit an individual gets); the risk that a blunt "AI-assisted" label creates stigma or interacts with existing Global-South publishing biases, arguing for granular disclosure instead; and a taxonomy with clearer, non-overlapping categories (e.g. "idea generation" is currently ambiguous) that can be compared to the CRediT contributor-role taxonomy. One proposal was a hybrid model: a machine-readable structured core for comparability, plus a short narrative field for context. On responsibility: since automated detection can't reliably reconstruct AI use after the fact, any standard will lean on honest researcher self-reporting, with fields for human verification/oversight rather than resolving legal/copyright questions directly. Planned output is two-level: minimum, a coordinated GFRN response to the ISC/WCRI Round 2 consultation before the October deadline; stretch, a consensus/position or research paper with a reproducibility-oriented taxonomy as an annex, potentially deposited on Zenodo or as a preprint (not to delay the consultation response). The companion workplan document opened sign-up for 1-2 topic leads per Round-2 topic plus a cross-cutting existing-standards/literature review; Oleksandr Berezko (UARN) has signed on to lead both the taxonomy topic and the cross-cutting review. Workflow: sign up → contribute (via a shared comments document) → synthesise (topic leads) → consolidate (topic leads + coordination team, led by Barbara Templ and Emmanuel Boakye) → whole-group review → submit → optionally extend into a public paper. Keywords: AI disclosure, reproducibility, ISC/WCRI, GFRN, reporting standard, research integrity, CRediT taxonomy. Related: [[Machine_Learning]], [[Professional_Background]].

- *Beyond FAIR Data: Making Research Software a First-Class Research Output* (`raw/2026-06-11-SNS-EELISA.pdf`): A 59-slide course/talk by Roberto Di Cosmo (Director, Software Heritage; Inria/Université Paris Cité), delivered 11 June 2026 — the filename suggests it was given for the EELISA European university alliance. Builds on a Nature commentary (Oct 2025, doi:10.1038/d41586-025-03196-0) calling for software to get the same archive/reference/citation/credit treatment as data, and frames the whole talk as "the how-to behind that call." Opens from Popper's reproducibility principle to argue that preserving source code itself — not just describing methods — is what makes computational research verifiable. Core technical content: the SWHID (Software Hash Identifier), an intrinsic, content-derived identifier built from a Merkle DAG of a project's full development history, which anyone can recompute offline to verify an archived copy matches the original ("a DOI answers 'which deposit?'; a SWHID answers 'exactly which bytes, and prove it'"); practical tooling for researchers — the CodeMeta Generator and an auto-derived `CITATION.cff` for describing software, and the HAL/Software Heritage curated-deposit workflow used in French research; adoption evidence, including a Spinellis et al. MSR 2026 dataset citing 30 SWHIDs and a population-genetics paper embedding SWHIDs since 2020. Closes on policy stakes: the 2019 EU Copyright Directive's "upload filter" rules, where Software Heritage helped secure a non-profit-archive exclusion, and the 2021 US Executive Order's software-supply-chain-security ("Know Your SoftWare") provisions — plus an infrastructure timeline from the archive's 2015 launch through its 2026 ENEA institutional mirror. Keywords: SWHID, CodeMeta, FAIR software, research software citation, Merkle DAG, software supply chain. Related: [[Professional_Background]], [[Data]].

- *A Reproducible, Pure-Python Connectivity Pipeline for a Data-Sparse Rewilding Landscape*: The Côa Valley Eco-Connectivity notebook (see [[Conservation]]) is explicitly framed, in its own draft paper abstract, as a reproducibility contribution rather than a methodological advance: a fully Python-based circuit-theory pipeline (`scipy.sparse` pairwise current-flow solving) built specifically so a resource-constrained conservation organization doesn't need a Julia runtime (as Circuitscape/Omniscape.jl requires) in its computing environment. The same abstract models reproducibility as including candid disclosure of a method's own limitations, not just runnable code — it reports the pipeline's Random Forest accuracy metrics as not out-of-sample validation, flags run-to-run stochasticity from an undocumented random seed, and notes a resistance-grid resampling step that dilutes fine-scale field-evidence effects. Source: `portfolio/paper-abstracts-draft.md` (abstract #1), `processed/portfolio/paper-abstracts-draft.md`.
